# State-Conditioned Rheology Enables Evidence-Grounded Formulation Decisions in Reactive Polyurethane Hot-Melt Adhesives

## Abstract

Reactive polyurethane hot-melt adhesives (PURs) are usually formulated and modeled from nominal composition and temperature, although the rheology encountered during processing is generated by a material state that may differ substantially between experimental realizations. Here we show that this realization dependence is highly structured within a local PPG2000/STEPANPOL PDP-70/4,4'-MDI formulation family and can be converted into an experimentally useful design representation. After chemistry-aware curation, six complete realizations comprising 36 temperature-viscosity measurements were analyzed. Nominally identical E2 formulations differed by up to 2.80–3.57-fold in viscosity across the measured 80–130 °C range, yet this variation was dominated by a nearly uniform shift in log viscosity. A formulation-only temperature model explained 85.2% of the log-viscosity variation, whereas a realization-specific scale plus shared thermal-response model explained 99.77%; held-temperature multiplicative error decreased from 1.442× to 1.058×. Model-free singular-value decomposition independently assigned 99.63% of between-realization variance to the first mode, whose cosine similarity to a constant vertical shift was 0.9998. This low-dimensional structure enabled one-point rheological state calibration: after withholding an entire nominal formulation, one viscosity anchor reconstructed the remaining local temperature response with approximately 1.06–1.10× pooled multiplicative error. In a stricter formulation-and-temperature holdout, a single 110 °C anchor predicted 120–130 °C viscosity with a pooled multiplicative RMSE of 1.088×. Thermal holding exposed a different design coordinate: apparent log-viscosity drift at 120 °C differed 4.29-fold between two original formulations. We therefore represent local reactive-PUR rheology by a realized viscosity state, a thermal-response coordinate, and a temporal trajectory rather than by one static viscosity target. These experimentally discovered rules were combined with curated external PUR evidence in an outcome-blind scientific Agent. On a 73-node target-blind formulation lattice that did not contain the held-out validation composition, all 8 committed Agent decisions entered the predeclared near region, compared with 0/7 for a single-pass LLM and 24.66% of nodes under uniform random selection. The transparent deterministic rule layer accounted for most of the quantitative improvement, showing that the useful decision geometry was supplied primarily by explicit scientific structure rather than unconstrained language-model inference. The resulting framework links low-dimensional state calibration, trajectory-aware formulation design, and outcome-blind experiment selection. Within the studied local chemistry family, it offers a route to reduce repeated full-curve characterization while directing experimental effort toward the process-state and thermal-hold coordinates that most strongly affect formulation reliability.

**Keywords:** reactive polyurethane hot-melt adhesive; rheology; process state; viscosity stability; state-aware modeling; formulation design; scientific Agent

## 1. Introduction

Reactive polyurethane hot-melt adhesives combine melt processability with subsequent chemical curing. They are applied in a fluid state, develop handling strength during cooling, and continue to evolve chemically after application, making processing rheology and later adhesive performance inseparable from formulation history [@Cui2002CrystallineStructure; @Cui2003CureKinetics; @OrgilesCalpena2016CO2HMPUR; @Blasco2022VegetablePolyols; @MoyanoVallejo2024GreenStrength]. The useful processing window is therefore not defined by one viscosity value alone. The material must remain sufficiently fluid for melting, pumping, coating, or dispensing, while also remaining sufficiently stable during its residence at elevated temperature and retaining the reactivity required for later cure.

Polyurethane-prepolymer viscosity is known to depend on temperature, soft-segment chemistry, molecular weight, stoichiometry, and reaction progress [@Sebenik2007SoftSegment; @ParutaTuarez2014SystemicRheology; @Ruan2019BioPolyols; @Liu2020PPCPUR; @Pugar2025PURViscosityML]. Reaction temperature can alter the molecular-weight distribution and side-reaction landscape of prepolymer formation, and the miscibility of reactive blends can itself evolve with extent of reaction [@Heintz2003ReactionTemperature; @Duffy2005ReactiveBlendMiscibility]. These observations create a data-modeling problem that is especially important in formulation science: nominal composition is often recorded much more consistently than reaction history, thermal residence, sample age, mixing trajectory, moisture exposure, or other aspects of the material state realized in a specific preparation. A formulation database may therefore represent one recipe as though it possessed one deterministic viscosity-temperature curve even when separate realizations occupy measurably different rheological states.

This distinction is more consequential than ordinary measurement noise. If formulation identity uniquely determined the rheological response, repeated realizations would fluctuate around one composition-defined curve. If, instead, realization history introduces a structured displacement of the curve, then the displacement itself becomes a state coordinate that can be measured, modeled, and used experimentally. The relevant question is therefore not simply whether process history matters—this is already established in reactive polyurethane chemistry—but whether realization-dependent variability has a low-dimensional form that can be exploited for formulation design.

A second limitation of static formulation models is that they often reduce processing behavior to a single viscosity target. For a reactive hot melt, however, an acceptable instantaneous viscosity does not imply that the material will remain within the same processing window during thermal holding. Previous HMPUR studies show that formulation changes can shift melt viscosity, viscoelastic response, open time, green strength, and thermal performance by different amounts rather than moving these properties in lockstep [@Jung2008AcrylicModification; @Sun2016PEDA; @Sun2022PolycarbonatePolyester; @Sun2022PolycarbonateSebacicPUR; @MoyanoVallejo2024GreenStrength]. Temperature response and time-at-temperature stability should therefore be treated as potentially distinct formulation coordinates rather than collapsed into one scalar rheological objective.

Recent work has demonstrated that temperature-dependent polyurethane-prepolymer viscosity can be represented effectively with physically informed curve parameters and chemistry-aware machine-learning models. Pugar et al. analyzed 39 prepolymers and showed that Andrade-type temperature-response representations offer an interpretable description of continuous viscosity-temperature behavior, while chemistry-to-viscosity prediction remains strongest within the chemical domains represented during training [@Pugar2025PURViscosityML]. This provides a broad-chemistry context for a complementary local question: once a narrow formulation family is fixed, how much of the remaining variability belongs to the realized material state, and can that state be calibrated with less experimental information than a complete temperature sweep?

The present study addresses this problem through a discovery-to-decision workflow. We first analyze a local five-formulation PUR design and ask whether repeated temperature-sweep realizations collapse onto a shared thermal-response shape after a realization-specific viscosity shift is removed. We then test whether this low-dimensional representation transfers across nominal formulations through one-point state calibration, including a stricter case in which both a formulation and a high-temperature prediction region are withheld. In parallel, we quantify thermal-hold drift as a separate rheological trajectory and compare its formulation sensitivity with the local thermal-response descriptor.

The local experiment identifies the physical failure mode but is too sparse to uniquely determine an intervention outside the original PPG2000/PDP-70/MDI formulation axes. We therefore use a curated PUR evidence base to define chemically defensible resin-modification directions and to establish the broader range over which temperature-response behavior changes across polyurethane chemistry. External evidence is treated as a prior over plausible formulation regions rather than as a direct predictor of local wet-lab performance.

Finally, the experimentally discovered rheological rules and the curated formulation priors are exposed to a scientific Agent through deterministic tools. The Agent is not used to invent physical laws or replace laboratory measurements. Its role is to decide which experiment is justified under sparse evidence, explicit uncertainty, and constrained chemical search. Decision quality is evaluated by an outcome-blind reconstruction in which the later validation formulation and measurements are structurally inaccessible until all recommendations have been frozen. This design follows the broader tool-grounded and closed-loop direction of chemistry AI and autonomous experimentation [@Hase2019SelfDrivingLabs; @Roch2018ChemOS; @Boiko2023Coscientist; @Bran2024ChemCrow] while placing the material-science result, rather than the language model, at the center of the workflow. Related autonomous platforms have shown how algorithmic experiment selection can be coupled to robotic or high-throughput execution, active learning, and accumulated scientific knowledge [@Burger2020MobileRoboticChemist; @MacLeod2020SelfDrivingLab; @Kusne2020ClosedLoopMaterials; @Stach2021AutonomousExperimentation; @Szymanski2023AutonomousLab].

The central hypothesis is that reactive-PUR rheology within a local chemistry family is more usefully represented as a realized rheological state plus response coordinates than as a unique composition-to-viscosity mapping. We test whether: (i) realization-dependent differences are dominated by a viscosity-scale coordinate superimposed on a transferable thermal-response shape; (ii) one measurement can locate an unseen local realization on that shape; (iii) thermal-hold drift constitutes a separate, more strongly formulation-sensitive trajectory in the measured local contrast; and (iv) these physical regularities can be converted into an auditable formulation decision under sparse evidence.

## 2. Results and Discussion

### 2.1 Nominally identical formulations occupy different realized rheological states

The local experimental design contained five nominal formulations based on PPG2000, STEPANPOL PDP-70, and 4,4'-MDI. E1-E3 used a 50/50 PPG2000/PDP-70 polyol ratio and varied the reported NCO:OH ratio from 1.70 to 1.90. E4 and E5 retained NCO:OH = 1.80 while shifting the PPG2000/PDP-70 ratio to 60/40 and 40/60, respectively. Complete 80–130 °C temperature sweeps were available for E1-E3 across multiple experimental realizations.

The most immediate result was that nominal formulation identity did not uniquely determine absolute viscosity. Three recorded E2 realizations, all corresponding to the same nominal recipe, gave viscosities of 9462, 18780, and 27350 in source-reported units at 80 °C and 1955, 4017, and 6977 at 120 °C. The corresponding maximum-to-minimum ratios were 2.89× and 3.57×. Across the six measured temperatures, the E2 realization spread remained between approximately 2.80× and 3.57×.

This observation is not interpreted as evidence that nominal composition is irrelevant. Rather, it demonstrates that composition alone is insufficient to identify the rheological state realized in the experiment. The same nominal recipe can occupy substantially different viscosity levels after preparation. Because these differences persist over the full measured temperature range, they are inconsistent with an isolated single-temperature outlier and instead suggest a systematic shift of the temperature-viscosity curve.

The correct physical question is therefore whether the realizations differ arbitrarily or whether their differences can be represented by a low-dimensional state coordinate. This distinction matters for design. Unstructured variation must be handled primarily as uncertainty; structured variation can potentially be calibrated.

### 2.2 Realization variability collapses onto a viscosity-scale coordinate

We compared two representations of the chemistry-audited temperature-sweep data. A formulation-only description treats nominal chemical identity and temperature as the primary predictors,

[
ln eta = f(x_{mathrm{chem}},T)+arepsilon.
]

A state-conditioned representation instead allows each experimental realization to occupy its own viscosity-scale coordinate while sharing a common local thermal-response function,

[
ln eta_r(T)=alpha_r+g(T)+arepsilon,
]

where (alpha_r) is the realization-specific offset in log viscosity.

After chemistry-aware curation, the primary dataset contained 36 observations from six complete realizations of three nominal formulations. One E1 temperature curve labelled with phosphoric-acid context was retained for sensitivity analysis but excluded from the primary state model because the additive condition was not represented in the compact formulation definition.

The formulation-only model explained 85.2% of the variation in log viscosity. Introducing realization-specific viscosity-scale terms with a shared low-complexity inverse-temperature response increased the explained variation to 99.77%. The improvement remained when prediction rather than in-sample fit was examined. Under held-temperature validation, the formulation-only model produced a multiplicative error factor of approximately 1.442×, whereas the state-conditioned model gave approximately 1.058×.

The result is therefore not simply that a model with more parameters fits better. The additional degree of freedom has a specific geometric interpretation: most of the disagreement among experimental realizations is a displacement of the viscosity level, not a complete restructuring of the temperature response.

A model-free singular-value decomposition supported the same conclusion. After centering the log-viscosity matrix by temperature, the first between-realization mode explained approximately 99.63% of the variance. The loading vector of this mode had a cosine similarity of approximately 0.9998 to a constant vector. Thus, the dominant realization-to-realization mode is almost indistinguishable from a uniform vertical shift in log-viscosity space.

This convergence between regression and model-free decomposition is central to the physical interpretation. Within the tested local chemistry family, realization-dependent rheology is well approximated by a shared thermal-response shape whose position is shifted by a latent material-state coordinate. The state coordinate should not be assigned to one unmeasured cause. The available data do not separately identify reaction time, water content, mixing trajectory, sample age, or other preparation variables. We therefore treat (alpha_r) as a latent realized-state descriptor rather than as a mechanistic label.

This distinction also clarifies what the E2 spread proves. It does not, by itself, establish a specific non-additive molecular mechanism or a failure of a particular polyol mixing law. It directly establishes that the realized rheological level is not uniquely fixed by the nominal formulation record.

### 2.3 One viscosity measurement can calibrate an unseen local realization

A useful state representation should reduce experimental burden, not merely summarize completed measurements. We therefore tested whether the shared thermal-response shape could be transferred to a nominal formulation that was absent from model fitting.

In a leave-one-formulation-out analysis, all realizations of one formulation were removed. The shared thermal-response function was estimated from the remaining formulations. For each held realization, only one measured viscosity value was then supplied as an anchor, providing the realization-specific offset,

[
alpha_r=lneta_r(T_0)-g(T_0),
]

after which the remaining temperatures were reconstructed from the learned shape.

With 120 °C as the anchor, the multiplicative reconstruction errors were approximately 1.028× for held E1, 1.119× for held E2, and 1.049× for held E3. Pooled across held realizations, the error was approximately 1.099×. Across the available anchor temperatures, pooled error remained approximately 1.06–1.10×.

We then imposed a stricter test in which the target formulation and the high-temperature prediction region were both excluded from thermal-shape fitting. For each fold, one formulation was removed entirely, and the shared shape was fitted using only the remaining formulations at temperatures up to 110 °C. A single 110 °C viscosity from each unseen realization was supplied as its state anchor, and the model predicted the unseen 120 °C and 130 °C responses.

Across 12 held predictions from six realizations, the pooled multiplicative RMSE was 1.088×. Errors were 1.087× at 120 °C and 1.089× at 130 °C, with a median absolute percentage error of 5.68%. A realization-level cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples gave a 95% interval of approximately 1.043–1.126× for pooled multiplicative RMSE.

This stress test remains local: the extrapolation spans only 10–20 °C beyond the fitting region and remains inside the audited E1-E3 chemistry neighborhood. It does not establish a universal polyurethane master curve. Its significance is experimental. Once a family-level thermal shape has been learned, a new local realization need not be characterized by a full sweep simply to locate its viscosity scale. One state-specific measurement can calibrate the realization and recover the remaining temperature response with substantially lower error than nominal formulation identity alone.

We refer to this strategy as **one-point rheological state calibration**. It separates two experimental tasks that are usually conflated: learning the transferable thermal response of a local chemistry family, and locating the current physical realization on that response. The second task can be accomplished with substantially less information than a complete temperature sweep.

### 2.4 Thermal-hold stability defines a separate formulation-sensitive trajectory

The shared temperature-response shape does not imply that all processing behavior can be reduced to (alpha_r+g(T)). Reactive hot melts can continue to evolve at constant temperature, and this temporal trajectory is directly relevant to processing stability.

The local apparent temperature-sensitivity descriptor was comparatively concentrated across the chemistry-audited temperature sweeps. Regression of (lneta) against (1/T) gave a mean apparent (E_eta) of approximately 42.05 kJ mol(^{-1}), with a standard deviation of approximately 2.43 kJ mol(^{-1}) and a coefficient of variation of approximately 5.77%. Here (E_eta) is used only as a compact rheological descriptor of temperature response; it is not interpreted as a chemical reaction activation energy.

The 120 °C thermal-hold trajectories showed much stronger formulation dependence. For E1, the source-reported viscosity increased from 708.7 at 15 min to 776.1 at 60 min and 828.1 at 90 min. For E5, viscosity increased from 2210 to 3349 and 4267 over the same times. The directly observed 15–60 min increases were therefore 9.51% and 51.54%, respectively.

A descriptive log-viscosity drift model,

[
ln eta(t)=lneta_0+k_{mathrm{drift}}t,
]

gave (k_{mathrm{drift}}approx0.125~mathrm{h}^{-1}) for E1 and (0.537~mathrm{h}^{-1}) for E5, a 4.29-fold difference. E1 and E5 differ in both composition and stoichiometry, so this contrast does not isolate one causal formulation axis. It does establish that the temporal response can change substantially within the local design even while the temperature-response descriptor remains comparatively concentrated.

The local rheological design object is therefore better written as

[
mathbf{R}_{mathrm{rheo}}
=
{eta_{mathrm{ref}},,S_T,,S_t},
]

where (eta_{mathrm{ref}}) locates the realized viscosity scale, (S_T) describes the local thermal response, and (S_t) describes the time-dependent trajectory under isothermal holding. These coordinates are not claimed to be universally independent or orthogonal. The present result is narrower: they display different sensitivities within the measured local formulation space and therefore should not be collapsed into a single static viscosity target.

This representation also changes how experimental variability should be interpreted. A change in (eta_{mathrm{ref}}) answers where the realized sample lies in rheological space. (S_T) answers how that state moves with temperature. (S_t) answers how it evolves with time under processing conditions. Formulation design controls not only a point in property space, but also a trajectory through it.

### 2.5 External evidence defines both the boundary of the local model and the next chemical search direction

The local state model is intentionally restricted to a narrow chemistry family. The broader PUR evidence base provides an important boundary on any attempt to generalize the shared thermal response.

The external database contains 39 dense polyurethane-prepolymer temperature-viscosity curves comprising 4559 individual measurements. Reanalysis of these curves gave a median (R^2) of approximately 0.9967 for (lneta) versus (1/T), with 37 of 39 curves at (R^2geq0.98). The corresponding apparent temperature-response descriptors span approximately 34.7–94.2 kJ mol(^{-1}). The local value near 42 kJ mol(^{-1}) is therefore not a universal polyurethane constant. Instead, the broader data support a useful two-scale interpretation: the thermal-response shape is transferable inside the present local chemistry neighborhood but remains chemistry-dependent across a broader PUR landscape [@Pugar2025PURViscosityML].

The external evidence base also serves a second purpose. The original E1-E5 design can diagnose realization-state sensitivity and thermal-hold instability, but it contains no resin-modified formulation axis. Local data alone therefore cannot identify whether the next experiment should introduce an acrylic-like resin, a tackifier-like component, or another chemical intervention outside the measured reactive core.

Published PUR studies provide evidence that acrylic, organoclay-containing, and other polymeric modifiers can alter melt viscosity, viscoelastic response, set behavior, green strength, and adhesive performance [@Jeong2007Organoclay; @Jung2008AcrylicModification; @Kim2008AcrylicCopolymerMMT; @Cho2009AcrylicNanocomposite; @Kim2009MolecularWeightOrganoclay; @Ruan2021PolyacrylatePURHMA]. Related work also shows that modifier loading and soft-segment chemistry can reorganize the balance among rheology, open time, mechanical response, hydrolytic resistance, and thermal behavior across polyether-, polyester-, polycarbonate-, bio-based-, and high-temperature-oriented PUR formulations [@Ruan2019BioPolyols; @Liu2020PPCPUR; @OrgilesCalpena2016CO2HMPUR; @Blasco2022VegetablePolyols; @Sun2016PEDA; @Sun2022PolycarbonatePolyester; @Sun2022PolycarbonateSebacicPUR; @MoyanoVallejo2024GreenStrength; @Xiao2025HighTemperaturePUR; @Fang2026FDCAPURHMA].

These records are used as **formulation priors**, not as direct property predictors. Modifier percentages are compared numerically only when their denominator basis is sufficiently clear. Records with ambiguous addition bases are retained as directional evidence rather than treated as precise total-formulation targets. The role of the database is therefore to define chemically defensible intervention families and broad search regions that are absent from the sparse local design.

This division of labor is important. Local experiment supplies the physical diagnosis. The external evidence supplies plausible chemical directions. Neither is asked to do the other's job.

### 2.6 The physical discovery converts formulation design from property prediction to decision under sparse evidence

The local dataset is deliberately small: five nominal formulations, six chemistry-audited complete temperature realizations, and two original thermal-hold trajectories. This is sufficient to expose structure, but not sufficient to train a credible black-box predictor from formulation composition to thermal-hold drift across a new modifier chemistry.

Experimental allocation under such sparse, expensive conditions is closely related to the broader problem of choosing informative experiments rather than exhaustively sampling a chemical space [@Raccuglia2016FailedExperiments; @Shields2021BayesianReactionOptimization].

The next formulation task is therefore not

[
x_{mathrm{chem}}ightarrow widehat{k}_{mathrm{drift}},
]

followed by numerical optimization over a poorly supported predictor. It is a decision problem:

> given a measured failure mode, a low-dimensional local rheological state model, incomplete process-state knowledge, and external evidence for untested intervention families, which experiment is scientifically justified next?

The scientific Agent was constructed around this formulation. The upstream material analysis is exposed through deterministic tools rather than summarized only in natural-language prompts. The principal state-aware rheology tool provides the formulation-only versus state-conditioned model comparison, the model-free state-shift result, one-point calibration performance, the local thermal-response descriptor, the E1/E5 thermal-hold contrast, and explicit boundaries on what these results support.

External evidence is supplied through a separate evidence layer. The hierarchy is therefore

[
	ext{local experiment}
ightarrow
	ext{physical rules}
ightarrow
	ext{external formulation priors}
ightarrow
	ext{constraints and uncertainty}
ightarrow
	ext{experiment decision}.
]

The multi-stage Agent follows Planner → Evidence/Tool Layer → Proposer → Skeptic → Robustness Adjudicator → Judge → Freeze. These stages are used to separate problem definition, evidence retrieval, candidate generation, criticism, uncertainty handling, and final commitment. The Agent may also abstain when the available evidence does not justify a recommendation.

This architecture is not presented as evidence that every software module is individually necessary. Its purpose is to make the decision trace auditable and to keep language-model judgment downstream of experimentally grounded structure.

### 2.7 Outcome-blind reconstruction tests decision quality without exposing the answer

The original contemporaneous machine-readable recommendation artifact was not retained. We therefore do not claim that the current software is an exact replay of a historical Agent that generated the later validation formulation. Instead, decision quality is evaluated through a stricter outcome-blind reconstruction.

Benchmark design is treated as part of the scientific method rather than as an afterthought, consistent with prior work emphasizing realistic experiment-planning benchmarks and explicit performance metrics for autonomous laboratories [@Hase2021Olympus; @Volk2024SelfDrivingLabMetrics]. The scored benchmark uses a 73-node candidate lattice generated from the original local design and external evidence that was independent of the later validation outcome. The held-out validated formulation is deliberately not a node in the lattice, making exact recipe recovery impossible by construction. The nearest available lattice point lies 1.877 percentage points away in the modifier plane.

A structural blindness audit verifies that the held-out formulation identity, follow-up thermal-hold measurements, derived post-result statistics, and adjudication labels are inaccessible to the Agent runtime. Direct action requests targeting the held-out formulation are blocked by the evidence firewall. Candidate identifiers contain no rank or historical identity, and no scored candidate includes the later measurement schedule.

The benchmark chronology is programmatically enforced:

[
	ext{pre-result evidence}
ightarrow
	ext{Agent decision}
ightarrow
	ext{freeze and hash}
ightarrow
	ext{blind phase closed}
ightarrow
	ext{held-out truth loaded}
ightarrow
	ext{adjudication}.
]

On the confirmatory series, 10 runs were attempted. The Agent committed to a candidate in 8 runs and abstained in 2. All 8 committed decisions fell within the predeclared near region of the held-out formulation. Their mean modifier-plane (L_1) distance was 2.281 percentage points and the median was 1.877 percentage points, equal to the lattice construction floor.

The near-region endpoint is substantially stricter than asking whether the Agent merely selected both modifier axes. Only 18 of 73 frozen candidates, or 24.66%, fall inside the near region, whereas 48 of 73 contain nonzero values on both modifier axes. Uniform random selection therefore enters the near region with probability 24.66% and has a mean modifier-plane (L_1) distance of 12.074 percentage points.

A naive single-pass LLM baseline behaved differently. Across seven valid decisions it produced 0/7 near-region selections and repeatedly returned to a reactive-core-only candidate rather than leaving the originally measured chemistry. The difference is therefore not simply that the full system samples more diverse answers; the structured workflow changes the decision region.

Attribution analysis further showed that most of the numerical improvement did not arise from unconstrained language-model inference. Re-engineering the transparent deterministic rule layer moved the best rule-based candidate from 15.115 to 2.615 percentage points from the held-out formulation, accounting for approximately 94% of the total distance improvement. To determine whether the language model simply copied this ranking, the confirmatory Agent was run without exposing the precomputed deterministic ordering. In 7 of 8 committed runs it departed from the hidden deterministic rank-1 while remaining concentrated in the evidence-supported region.

The appropriate interpretation is therefore not that the LLM independently discovered the experimental recipe. The explicit scientific rules define most of the useful decision geometry; the language-model layer contributes a smaller decision step within that constrained region. This attribution is a positive property of the workflow because scientific reliability should come from visible evidence and rules rather than from opaque model preference.

### 2.8 Physical adjudication closes the discovery-to-decision loop

Only after the blind decision phase is closed is the completed validation experiment used as a physical yardstick.

The validation formulation contained PPG2000, PDP-70, AC1920, TK100, and MDI at source-reported parts of 39.60, 39.60, 17, 5, and 20.19, respectively. Two independent thermal-hold runs were recorded at 120 °C over 15–60 min. The first changed from 1230 at 15 min to 1228 at 60 min, corresponding to -0.16%. The second changed from 1281 to 1320, corresponding to +3.04%. The mean profile changed by +1.47%.

These results are substantially flatter than the original E1 and E5 responses over the same 15–60 min interval, which increased by 9.51% and 51.54%, respectively. The validation experiment therefore occupies a low-drift region relative to the original local references.

The present data establish rheological stabilization, not a unique molecular mechanism. The follow-up formulation reduces the proportion of the original reactive polyol-rich composition and introduces resin-like components, and this change is consistent with a lower effective concentration of reaction-capable material during thermal holding. However, no direct NCO-consumption kinetics, conversion-resolved spectroscopy, molecular-weight time series, or chain-level structural characterization was measured. The manuscript therefore treats the mechanism as an interpretation rather than as a demonstrated molecular pathway.

Taken together, the experiments support a state-conditioned formulation framework,

[

]

where (x_{mathrm{chem}}) records nominal chemistry, (z_{mathrm{state}}) denotes the realized material/process state, (mathbf{R}_{mathrm{rheo}}) contains rheological level, thermal response, and temporal trajectory, and (U) records uncertainty and evidence limitations.

The resulting workflow is not an autonomous formulation generator. It is a closed scientific sequence:

The materials result therefore determines what the Agent is allowed to optimize, while the Agent provides an auditable interface for choosing a useful next experiment when direct property prediction is not justified by the available data.


### 2.9 Design implications: calibrate state, measure trajectory, and let AI choose evidence-bounded experiments

The combined results suggest a different experimental logic from the usual composition-to-property workflow. The realization-specific offset \\(\\alpha_r\\) should not automatically be discarded as nuisance variance. Within the present chemistry family, it behaves as a structured latent state coordinate that can be located experimentally. Once the family-level thermal-response function has been learned, one viscosity anchor provides a practical estimate of that coordinate and substantially reduces the information required to reconstruct the local temperature response. The immediate implication is not that one measurement universally replaces rheological characterization, but that **full temperature sweeps need not be repeated for every closely related realization when the local shared-shape assumption has already been validated**.

This distinction separates characterization into two tasks. The first is **state calibration**: determining where a realized sample lies on the local viscosity scale. The second is **trajectory assessment**: determining how that state changes under the processing history that matters operationally. The thermal-hold experiments show why these tasks cannot be collapsed. A formulation can be located accurately on the temperature-response surface and still follow an undesirable time-dependent trajectory at fixed temperature. For reactive PUR design, the experimentally relevant object is therefore not only a point in rheological space but the path followed by that point during processing.

The same logic determines the appropriate role of AI. The present workflow is not a conventional black-box property predictor and is not claimed to constitute a complete iterative active-learning loop. It is better described as **evidence-grounded active experimental decision-making**: experimentally discovered physical coordinates define what must be measured, the external PUR evidence base defines chemically plausible intervention regions, deterministic rules and constraints define the admissible decision geometry, and the language model helps choose and justify the next experiment inside that evidence-bounded space.

This interpretation is reinforced by the Agent attribution result. Approximately 94% of the quantitative distance improvement arose from the transparent deterministic rule layer, whereas the language-model layer contributed a smaller decision step after the precomputed ranking was withheld. Rather than diminishing the role of the Agent, this result clarifies where scientific reliability should come from: physical structure and traceable evidence should carry most of the decision burden, while the language model integrates, critiques, and acts within those constraints.

For industrial formulation development, the practical opportunity is therefore more specific than a generic claim of faster AI-driven optimization. In a validated local chemistry family, one-point state calibration can reduce repeated full-curve characterization, while explicit thermal-hold measurements concentrate laboratory effort on the dynamic coordinate that static viscosity cannot reveal. The Agent then provides an auditable mechanism for deciding which formulation/process region is worth testing next and for abstaining when the evidence is insufficient. The present study does not quantify total development-cycle reduction or universal process robustness; it establishes the experimentally grounded components needed for such a workflow.

## 3. Materials and Methods

### 3.1 Local formulation design

The original formulation space contained five reactive PUR compositions based on PPG2000, STEPANPOL PDP-70, and 4,4'-MDI. E1-E3 used a 50/50 PPG2000/PDP-70 polyol ratio with reported NCO:OH values of 1.70, 1.80, and 1.90. E4 and E5 retained NCO:OH = 1.80 while using PPG2000/PDP-70 ratios of 60/40 and 40/60. Formulation records are stored in the versioned repository data tables.

The validation formulation contained PPG2000, PDP-70, AC1920, TK100, and MDI on a source-reported parts basis. Because the source record did not provide a verified NCO:OH value for the validation formulation, no stoichiometric ratio was reconstructed.

### 3.2 Temperature-sweep data and chemistry audit

Temperature-sweep viscosity was recorded from 80 to 130 °C in 10 °C increments. The absolute viscosity unit is not confirmed in the available source record; local values are therefore reported as source-reported viscosity rather than assigned an unverified unit.

Run identifiers were retained for provenance. Project metadata confirms that R01, R02, and R03 are realization labels associated with the same operator rather than different operator identities.

One E1 temperature curve was labelled with phosphoric-acid context. Because the additive condition was not represented in the compact formulation table and its exact amount was not encoded, this curve was excluded from the chemistry-audited primary state analysis and retained for sensitivity analysis. The resulting primary temperature-sweep dataset contained 36 observations from six complete realizations of three nominal formulations.

### 3.4 Model-free dimensionality analysis

For the six chemistry-audited complete curves, a matrix of log viscosity was assembled with realizations as rows and temperatures as columns. Each temperature column was centered across realizations. Singular-value decomposition was then applied to the centered matrix.

The fraction of between-realization variance explained by the first singular mode was calculated from the corresponding singular value. To determine whether this mode represented a uniform log-viscosity shift, its loading vector was compared with a constant vector by cosine similarity.

### 3.5 One-point state calibration

Transfer across nominal formulations was evaluated by leave-one-formulation-out analysis. For each fold, all realizations of one formulation were excluded from fitting the shared thermal shape. The remaining formulations were used to estimate (g(T)). One viscosity value from each held realization at anchor temperature (T_0) was then used to estimate

[
alpha_r=lneta_r(T_0)-g(T_0).
]

The remaining temperatures were reconstructed from (alpha_r+g(T)). Performance was summarized in multiplicative-error space.

A stricter formulation-and-temperature holdout removed one formulation entirely and fitted the shared thermal response only on the other formulations at temperatures (leq110 ^circmathrm{C}). A single 110 °C anchor was then provided for each unseen realization, and predictions were generated at 120 and 130 °C. Pooled log-RMSE, multiplicative RMSE, absolute percentage error, and a 10,000-replicate realization-level cluster bootstrap were calculated.

### 3.7 Apparent temperature-response descriptor

For each chemistry-audited complete realization, (lneta) was regressed against (1/T). The fitted slope was converted to an apparent (E_eta) using the gas constant. (E_eta) is reported only as a descriptor of the local temperature-viscosity response.

### 3.8 External PUR evidence base

The external evidence layer contains literature, patent, material, formulation, measurement, and dense viscosity-curve records with explicit provenance. The dense prepolymer set comprises 39 temperature-viscosity curves and 4559 individual measurements.

External formulation records containing acrylic-like or tackifier-like components were used as evidence for candidate-space construction. Numeric modifier fractions were used as anchors only when the denominator basis was sufficiently clear. Records with unresolved fraction definitions were retained as directional evidence. External records were not used as direct predictors of local validation performance.

### 3.9 Scientific Agent

The scientific Agent operates downstream of the physical analysis. The canonical decision sequence is Planner → Evidence/Tool Layer → Proposer → Skeptic → Robustness Adjudicator → Judge → Freeze.

The Evidence/Tool layer exposes deterministic summaries of the local state-conditioned rheology, one-point calibration, thermal-response descriptor, thermal-hold contrast, candidate evidence, formulation composition, process uncertainty, and external analogue support. The Proposer ranks experiment candidates by scientific usefulness. The Skeptic attempts to identify evidence misuse, unsupported mechanism claims, process-state ambiguity, or leakage. The Robustness Adjudicator evaluates whether the proposal remains useful under plausible uncertainty and alternative evidence priorities. The Judge commits to a performance candidate, robustness probe, uncertainty probe, or abstention.

Freeze is programmatic. The frozen record stores candidate identity, rationale, uncertainty decomposition, acceptance criterion, timestamps, model identifiers, and cryptographic hashes of relevant inputs and prompts.

### 3.10 Outcome-blind benchmark and evidence firewall

The scored Agent benchmark uses a target-blind runtime profile. The held-out validation formulation, its follow-up measurements, derived post-result statistics, and adjudication labels are removed before model payloads are assembled. Action calls targeting the blinded formulation are blocked by the evidence firewall.

The candidate space contains 73 nodes generated from the original local design and pre-result external evidence. The held-out validation composition is not a candidate node. Eighteen of 73 candidates lie within the predeclared near region used for post-unblinding adjudication.

Each benchmark series is completed before the held-out truth can be loaded. Recommendations are frozen and hashed, a blind-phase closure record is written, and the adjudicator verifies the frozen recommendation hash before scoring. The primary confirmatory series contained 10 runs under an identical target-blind evidence contract.

The naive single-pass LLM baseline used the same pre-result evidence but did not use the full structured decision workflow. Uniform random selection over the same 73-node lattice provides an exact search-space baseline.

### 3.11 Statistical scope

The local formulation and realization counts are limited. The primary conclusions therefore rely on convergent evidence rather than on one significance test: chemistry-aware data curation, held-temperature prediction, model-free dimensionality analysis, leave-one-formulation calibration, bounded formulation-and-temperature holdout, and matched-window thermal-hold measurements.

Claims are restricted to the local formulation family unless supported by the broader external database. The local shared thermal-response shape is not generalized across unrelated PUR chemistry.

## 4. Conclusions

This study identifies a low-dimensional structure in the realization-dependent rheology of a local reactive-PUR chemistry family and shows that the structure is experimentally actionable. Nominally identical formulations differed by up to approximately threefold in absolute viscosity, yet these differences were not arbitrary: a realization-specific viscosity-scale coordinate superimposed on a shared local thermal-response shape explained 99.77% of the log-viscosity variation, while a model-free decomposition assigned 99.63% of between-realization variance to a near-uniform vertical shift. The direct conclusion is therefore not that nominal composition is unimportant, but that nominal composition alone does not uniquely specify the rheological state realized in the experiment.

The low-dimensional representation enables **one-point rheological state calibration**. After withholding an entire nominal formulation, one viscosity anchor located the held realization on the shared local thermal-response shape with approximately 1.06–1.10× pooled multiplicative error. In the stricter formulation-and-temperature holdout, a single 110 °C anchor supported prediction at 120–130 °C with a pooled multiplicative RMSE of 1.088×. This result does not establish a universal master curve across PUR chemistry. It establishes that, once a local chemistry-family response has been learned and validated, the realized state can be calibrated with substantially less information than a new complete temperature sweep.

Thermal holding exposes why state calibration alone is insufficient. The apparent 120 °C log-viscosity drift differed 4.29-fold between two original formulations, while the local temperature-response descriptor remained comparatively concentrated. We therefore distinguish three experimentally useful rheological coordinates: a realized viscosity level, a local thermal response, and a time-dependent processing trajectory. Formulation design determines not only where a material lies in rheological space, but also how that state evolves during thermal residence.

This physical representation changes the AI problem. The sparse E1–E5 dataset is sufficient to diagnose the local failure mode and state structure, but not to support a credible black-box predictor for a new modifier chemistry. External PUR evidence was therefore used to define chemically defensible intervention regions, while deterministic scientific tools encoded the experimentally discovered rheological rules. The Agent was asked to select a justified next experiment under sparse evidence rather than to hallucinate an unsupported final property.

Decision quality was evaluated through an outcome-blind reconstruction. The held-out validation formulation and its measurements were inaccessible during recommendation and the true composition was not present as a candidate node. All 8 committed Agent decisions entered the predeclared near region, compared with 0/7 decisions from a single-pass LLM and a 24.66% near-region fraction under uniform random selection over the same lattice. Approximately 94% of the quantitative distance improvement arose from the transparent deterministic rule layer. This attribution is a central methodological result: in scientific decision systems, explicit physical structure, evidence provenance, and constraints should carry most of the reliability, while the language model performs a narrower integration and decision function within that scientifically admissible space.

The industrial implication is therefore concrete but bounded. The framework does not yet demonstrate a universal reduction in formulation-development time, nor does it prove that one-point calibration can replace full rheological characterization across unrelated polyurethane chemistries. It does show how a validated local response shape can reduce repeated characterization burden, how thermal-hold trajectory can be separated from static viscosity, and how experimental resources can be directed toward high-information formulation/process regions rather than distributed uniformly across a large search space.

More broadly, the work suggests a state-conditioned formulation paradigm:

[
	ext{nominal chemistry}
ightarrow
	ext{realized-state calibration}
ightarrow

ightarrow
	ext{evidence-constrained next experiment}.
]

In this paradigm, AI is not a substitute for materials physics or wet-lab measurement. Its value is to make the transition from physical evidence to the next experimental decision explicit, auditable, and reproducible.
